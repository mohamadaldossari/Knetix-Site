How to Make Money in Sales With No Experience

By Mohamad Aldossari Jul 13, 2026 For Aspiring Reps 8 min read

Sales gets a bad rap. Say the word and people picture a pushy guy on a car lot or a cold-call boiler room. But strip away the stereotype and sales is one of the only fields left where you can make real money with no degree, no experience, and no connections, purely on effort. Nobody checks your resume when you close a deal.

The catch is that most people walk in with the wrong idea of what it actually takes, get discouraged in the first month, and quit right before it starts working. This is the honest version: how making money in sales really works when you're starting from zero, what actually makes someone good at it, and how to begin.

How commission sales actually works

Most sales roles pay one of two ways. There's salary plus commission, a base wage plus a cut of what you sell. It's steadier, but the base usually comes with a lower ceiling. Then there's commission-only, no base at all, just a percentage of every deal you close, with no cap. It sounds scarier, and in the early days it is, but it's where the real upside lives. You eat what you kill, and nobody puts a lid on how much you can earn.

Here's the honest tradeoff, because anyone who tells you it's easy money is lying. Commission-only isn't a guaranteed paycheck. It's higher-variance, slow at the start while you learn the product and build momentum, then compounding once it clicks. The reason most people never see the compounding is that they quit during the slow part. The ones who push through are exactly why the field pays so well: it filters for people who keep going.

The myth that's stopping you

The single biggest thing that keeps people out of sales is a myth: that you have to be a smooth, outgoing, silver-tongued talker. You don't. In fact, the flashy talkers are often the worst at it, because they talk when they should be listening.

The people who actually close, consistently, tend to share a very different set of traits. They show up every day. They ask questions and listen more than they pitch. They actually care whether the thing they're selling helps the person in front of them. And they don't take "no" personally. Notice that none of those require experience or a personality transplant, they're a matter of attitude and habit, both of which you can build starting today.

If you're more introverted, that can genuinely be an advantage. Listening is a superpower in sales, and quiet people are usually better at it. "Good with people" is overrated. Persistent and honest is underrated, and it wins.

What you actually need to start

Four things, and none of them is a background in sales.

  • A real product to sell that you believe in. Trying to sell something you secretly think is junk is miserable, and it shows. Sell something that genuinely helps people and the whole job gets easier.
  • Basic communication. If you can hold a conversation and send a clear text, you have enough to start. Everything else is a learnable skill, not a born talent.
  • A tolerance for "no." Sales is a numbers game. Every no gets you closer to a yes, and the people who make it are simply the ones who keep dialing after the tenth no.
  • Something to guide you. You don't have to invent the process from scratch. Scripts, a repeatable method, or someone who's done it before will save you months of flailing.

How to actually begin, from zero

Here's the path, in order.

1. Pick something real to sell

Ideally a product or service with recurring demand and obvious value, sold to people who actually need it. Local businesses are a great starting market, real owners, real problems, and decisions made fast, without the corporate committees and six-month sales cycles that make big-company selling brutal for a beginner.

2. Learn the product cold

You can't sell what you don't understand. Know it well enough to answer any question a customer throws at you without flinching. Confidence in sales comes from actually knowing your stuff, not from a pump-up speech.

3. Start reaching out, at volume

DMs, walk-ins, calls, whatever fits your market. Early on, volume is your friend, because you're learning as much as you're selling. The first handful of conversations feel awkward. That's completely normal, and it fades faster than you'd think.

4. Lead with their problem, not your pitch

Amateurs open by listing features. People who close open by asking what the person is struggling with, then showing how the thing they have solves it. Customers don't buy products. They buy solutions to problems they already have.

5. Follow up — that's where deals close

Most sales don't happen on the first touch. They happen on the second, third, or fourth. The person who follows up when everyone else gave up is the person who gets paid. Track your conversations so nobody falls through the cracks.

6. Don't take no personally

Some people just aren't a fit, and that's fine. A no isn't a verdict on you. It's information. Move on, keep going, and let the yeses come. Emotional resilience is half the job.

The fastest way in: get trained, don't wing it

You can do this the slow way, piece it together alone, or take a job somewhere that throws you on the floor with zero support and hopes you figure it out. Plenty of people do, and plenty of them burn out.

The fast way is to join something that trains you from the start, hands you a proven product and a real playbook, and pays you commission to sell it. You skip most of the trial-and-error and start earning while you learn, with a system behind you instead of a blank page.

The bottom line

Sales is one of the last real meritocracies. No degree, no pedigree, no permission needed, just effort, consistency, and a willingness to keep going after the word "no." You don't have to be a natural and you don't need experience. You need a real thing to sell, thick enough skin to hear no a lot, and the persistence to follow up when others quit. Start there, and the money follows.

Frequently asked

Can you really make money in sales with no experience?+

No. The myth that you need to be a slick, outgoing talker is the biggest thing that stops people. The best salespeople are usually consistent, genuine, and good listeners, introverts often do well precisely because they listen more than they talk. Being persistent and honest matters far more than being charismatic.
What's the difference between commission-only and salaried sales?+
Salaried sales pays a base plus commission, which is steadier but has a lower ceiling. Commission-only pays nothing up front but takes a percentage of every deal with no cap, so your income is tied directly to how much you close. Commission-only is higher-variance and higher-upside, which suits people willing to put in the work for uncapped earning.

Pick a real product you believe in, ideally one with recurring demand sold to people who genuinely need it. Learn it cold, start reaching out at volume, lead with the customer's problem instead of your pitch, and follow up, since most deals close on the follow-up. Is commission-only sales worth it?+
It's worth it if you're willing to push through a slow start. Early on it's harder than a wage because you're learning and building, but there's no ceiling, and once it clicks it compounds. Most people quit before that point, which is exactly why the ones who stick with it earn well.
